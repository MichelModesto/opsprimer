Most articles about CRMs for agencies compare feature grids. I want to show you
something more useful: what a CRM tells you about itself in the first hour, before
you've put a single real client in it.

I set one up from scratch and wrote down everything.

## The problem with picking a CRM as an agency

An agency isn't a sales team, and that turns out to matter more than any feature list.

A sales team has one pipeline, one product, and people whose whole job is moving
deals along it. An agency has several clients at different stages, work that
continues long after the deal closes, and — if you're small — one person doing
prospecting, delivery and invoicing in the same afternoon.

Almost every CRM is built for the first shape and sold to the second.

## Three questions that matter more than the feature list

### 1. Does the default setup match your size, or someone else's?

Every CRM ships with an opinion about how you work. You inherit that opinion on day
one, and most people never change it — they just work around it for years.

The tell is in the onboarding. If you say you're one person and it hands you a
seven-stage pipeline, that pipeline is going to sit half-empty forever, because
nobody drags a card through seven columns when they're also doing the work.

**The trade-off:** a CRM with strong opinions gets you running faster. A CRM with
weak opinions makes you design your own process before you've earned the right to
have one. Neither is free.

### 2. How much ships that you will never use?

This is the cost nobody quotes. Every field a CRM ships with shows up in your import
mapping, your filters, your form builder and your record views — forever. A tool
that arrives with a few hundred properties you don't need isn't neutral; it's a tax
on every screen you touch afterwards.

**The trade-off:** those fields exist because someone needed them. Stripping the
tool down means you'll hit a wall the day you need one of them back.

### 3. What happens at the edge of the free tier?

Free CRM tiers are real, and they're also a funnel. The question isn't whether the
free plan works — it's whether the first wall you hit is one you can live behind, or
one that forces an upgrade at the worst possible moment.

For an agency the walls that bite are usually: number of pipelines, number of
seats, and email sends. Not contact count, which is what the marketing pages lead with.

**The trade-off:** planning around the paid tier from day one costs money you may
not need to spend. Ignoring it costs a migration later, with client data in flight.

## What happened when I set up HubSpot as a one-person agency

I told it I was one person, in marketing and advertising, and that I wanted to track
leads from first contact to signed proposal.

### The AI onboarding doesn't check whether your input makes sense

Early on I pasted the wrong thing into the chat — a checklist, not a goal. It thought
for a second and answered as though I'd described a business objective, then carried
on building.

That's a small thing, but it tells you what the assistant is: a script that adapts
its wording, not something that validates what you gave it. Worth knowing before you
trust its output.

### It used my industry and ignored my size

This was the pattern, and it showed up twice.

**The pipeline it built had seven stages:** Prospecting, Initial Contact,
Qualification, Proposal Sent, Negotiation, Closed Won, Closed Lost. It specifically
justified "Proposal Sent" as an agency thing — which is fair, agencies do formalise
before negotiating.

But seven stages is a sales-team process. I had told it I was one person. A solo
operator works fine with four, and every stage past that is a card nobody moves.

**Then the dashboard it proposed had five reports, and three were about team
activity.** Team activity by date. Total team activities. Deals closed against
target — a target I had never set.

For a one-person operation, a team activity report is a chart of you versus you.

It knew my industry and used it well. It knew my headcount and did nothing with it.

### The properties it created are attributed to me, not to it

Four deal properties came out of the onboarding — campaign objective, media type,
campaign start date, campaign budget. Genuinely useful for agency work, and I'd have
built something similar by hand.

But in the properties list they're logged as created by me. Not by HubSpot, not by an
assistant. Six months from now, or when someone inherits this account, there is no
way to tell which decisions were mine and which came from a default.

It also created a property group I hadn't asked for.

None of that is harmful. It's just worth knowing that the audit trail doesn't
distinguish between what you chose and what was chosen for you.

### The bigger problem isn't what's missing — it's what's already there

Before I had a single contact, the account shipped with over two hundred contact
properties and nearly eighty deal properties.

Not all of them are noise; a lot exist for features I'm not using and some for
features the free plan doesn't include. But every one of them is in the dropdown
when you import a CSV, in the filter list when you build a segment, and in the field
picker when you make a form.

If importing a client list feels like more work than it should, this is why.

### One more thing worth flagging

The sample deal it created was assigned to Brian Halligan — HubSpot's co-founder —
sitting in the production pipeline as if it were real work. Harmless, and easy to
delete. But it's in there, and if you don't notice, it skews the first numbers you
look at.

## Who each one is genuinely for

### HubSpot

**Good fit if** you want a real CRM that costs nothing until you have volume, and
you'd rather delete what you don't need than build from an empty database.

**Skip it if** the branding on your forms and emails is a problem — on the free plan
it can't be removed — or if you know you'll need more than one pipeline soon, because
that's the wall you'll hit first.

## What I'd actually do

- **Just starting, no budget, want to learn what a CRM does:** HubSpot free. Delete
  the sample data first, and cut the pipeline down to four stages before you put
  anything real in it.
- **Email sequences are the core of the work:** ActiveCampaign, but price the entry
  tier honestly before you start — there's no free landing spot.
- **One person, under thirty deals, and the CRM is admin rather than strategy:**
  neither. Use something that lives in your inbox.
- **Multiple clients that must stay separated:** check the pipeline limit on
  whichever tier you can afford, before you migrate anything.

Whatever you pick, do the setup with fake data first and look at what the tool
assumes about you. That first hour tells you more than any comparison table — this
one included.
