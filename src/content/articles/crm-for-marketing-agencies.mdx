Most comparisons of CRMs for agencies are feature grids. Here's something more
useful: what a CRM assumes about you in the first hour, before a single real client
is in it.

I set one up from scratch and wrote down everything it decided on my behalf.

## Why an agency is a bad fit for most CRMs

A sales team has one pipeline, one product, and people whose entire job is moving
deals along it.

An agency has several clients at different stages, work that continues long after
the deal closes, and — if you're small — one person doing prospecting, delivery and
invoicing in the same afternoon.

Nearly every CRM is built for the first shape and sold to the second. The gap doesn't
show up in a feature comparison. It shows up in the defaults.

## Three questions worth more than the feature list

### 1. Does the default setup match your size, or someone else's?

Every CRM ships with an opinion about how you work. You inherit that opinion on day
one and most people never change it — they work around it for years instead.

The onboarding is the tell. If you say you're one person and it hands you a
seven-stage pipeline, that pipeline will sit half-empty forever, because nobody drags
a card through seven columns while also doing the actual work.

**What it costs either way:** a CRM with strong opinions gets you running fast. A CRM
with weak opinions makes you design a process before you've earned the right to have
one. Neither is free.

### 2. How much ships that you'll never use?

This is the cost nobody quotes. Every field a CRM ships with appears in your import
mapping, your filters, your form builder and every record view — permanently. A tool
that arrives with hundreds of properties you don't need isn't neutral. It's a tax on
every screen you touch afterwards.

**What it costs either way:** those fields exist because someone needed them. Strip
the tool down and you'll hit a wall the day you need one back.

### 3. Where's the first wall on the free tier?

Free CRM tiers are real, and they're also a funnel. The question isn't whether the
free plan works — it's whether the first wall you hit is one you can live behind, or
one that forces an upgrade at the worst possible moment.

For an agency the walls that bite are pipelines and seats. Not contact count, which
is what the pricing pages lead with.

## What HubSpot's setup decided for me

I told it I was one person, in marketing and advertising, and that I wanted to track
leads from first contact to signed proposal. An AI assistant handled the setup.

### It doesn't check whether your input makes sense

Early on I pasted the wrong text into the chat — a checklist, not a goal. It thought
for one second and replied as though I'd described a business objective, then carried
on building the workspace around it.

Small thing, but it tells you what the assistant is: a script that adapts its wording,
not something that validates what you gave it. Useful to know before you trust its
output.

### It used my industry and ignored my headcount

This was the pattern, and it appeared twice.

**The pipeline it built had seven stages:** Prospecting, Initial Contact,
Qualification, Proposal Sent, Negotiation, Closed Won, Closed Lost. It specifically
justified "Proposal Sent" as an agency need — fair, agencies do formalise before
negotiating.

But seven stages is a sales-team process with an SDR and a closer. I had told it I
was one person. A solo operator runs fine on four, and every stage beyond that is a
card nobody moves.

**Then the dashboard it proposed had five reports, and three were about team
activity:** team activities by date, total team activities, and deals closed against
target — a target I had never set.

For a one-person operation, a team activity report is a chart of you versus you.

It knew my industry and used it well. It knew my headcount and did nothing with it.

### The properties it created are logged under my name

Four deal properties came out of the setup: campaign objective, media type, campaign
start date, campaign budget. Genuinely useful for agency work — close to what I'd
have built by hand.

But in the properties list they're attributed to me. Not to HubSpot, not to an
assistant. Months from now, or when someone else inherits the account, there's no way
to tell which decisions were mine and which were defaults. It also created a property
group I hadn't asked for.

Not harmful. Just worth knowing that the audit trail doesn't distinguish between what
you chose and what was chosen for you.

### The real problem isn't what's missing — it's what's already there

Before I had a single contact, the account shipped with **213 contact properties and
77 deal properties.**

Not all of it is noise. Plenty exists for features I'm not using, and some for
features the free plan doesn't include at all. But every one of them sits in the
dropdown when you import a CSV, in the filter list when you build a segment, and in
the field picker when you make a form.

If importing a client list feels like more work than it should, that's why.

### Two smaller things

The sample deal it created was assigned to **Brian Halligan** — HubSpot's co-founder
— sitting in the production pipeline at $1,000 as though it were real work. Harmless
and easy to delete, but if you don't notice, it skews the first numbers you look at.

And the account set my number format to Brazil while leaving the timezone on US
Eastern. It took the country from the language and the timezone from the account
default without reconciling them. For anyone scheduling tasks or email sends, that's
the kind of mismatch that fires things at the wrong hour.

### The setup spends 40% of a quota you didn't know you had

The free plan allows **10 custom properties in total**. That number isn't shown
anywhere during onboarding.

The assistant created four of them on the deals object alone. So before I had touched
anything, 40% of the allowance was gone — spent on fields I hadn't asked for, however
sensible they were.

I only half-caught this at the time. I tried creating a property expecting to be
blocked, wasn't, and concluded there was no quota. Wrong conclusion: I was at four of
ten, with room left. The wall is real; I just hadn't reached it. The "10" I saw on the
creation screen was a different limit — unique-value properties — which is what
confused me.

Worth being precise about, because the practical advice depends on it: you can create
your own fields, but you're starting the race at 40% spent, and nothing tells you so.

## How the options compare

*Prices and limits verified on the official pricing pages. They change — confirm
before you commit.*

| | HubSpot | ActiveCampaign |
|---|---|---|
| Free plan | **Yes, permanent** — up to 2 users | **No** — 14-day trial only |
| Deal pipelines on free | **1** | n/a |
| Entry paid tier | Starter, **$7/seat/mo** | Starter |
| Pipelines on entry tier | **2** | **Not included** — CRM pipelines are a paid add-on |
| Built around | CRM first, marketing attached | Email automation first, light CRM |
| Where it hurts | Ships heavy; free tier carries HubSpot branding you can't remove | No free landing spot, and its entry tier doesn't do pipelines |

**How I'm using these words:** I set up and used HubSpot. I have not run ActiveCampaign
on real client work — its column comes from official documentation, not experience.
When that changes, this article changes.

## Who each is genuinely for

### HubSpot

**Good fit if** you want a real CRM that costs nothing until you have volume, and
you'd rather delete what you don't need than build from an empty database.

**Skip it if** branding on your forms and emails is a dealbreaker — on the free plan
it can't be removed — or if you'll need a second pipeline soon. That's the first wall,
and it arrives earlier than the contact limit.

[Try HubSpot's free CRM](https://www.hubspot.com/products/crm)

### ActiveCampaign

**Good fit if** the work is mostly email-led and the CRM exists to support sequences
rather than the other way round.

**Skip it if** you want to start at zero — there's no free tier to grow into, only a
trial. And check the pipeline question carefully: on the entry tier, deal pipelines
aren't part of the plan.

[ActiveCampaign](https://www.activecampaign.com/)

### The option I earn nothing from

If you're genuinely one person with fewer than about thirty active deals, a full
marketing CRM is probably the wrong tool. **Streak**, which lives inside Gmail, or
**OnePageCRM** will cost less and demand far less attention.

I haven't run either on client work — I'm flagging them from their documentation,
same caveat as above. I include them because for a real slice of readers they're the
better answer, and an article that only recommends what it earns from isn't worth
reading.

## What I'd actually do

- **Starting out, no budget, learning what a CRM does:** HubSpot free. Delete the
  sample deal first, then cut the pipeline to four stages before anything real goes in.
- **Email sequences are the core of the work:** ActiveCampaign — but price it honestly
  and confirm whether you need pipelines, because the entry tier doesn't include them.
- **One person, under thirty deals, CRM is admin rather than strategy:** neither. Use
  something that lives in your inbox.
- **Multiple clients that must stay separated:** check the pipeline limit on the tier
  you can actually afford, before you migrate anything. One pipeline means every
  client shares a board.

Whatever you pick, run the setup with fake data first and look hard at what the tool
assumed about you. That first hour tells you more than any comparison table — this one
included.
